We evaluate correctness through three metrics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Syntax rate (Syn.):} the percentage of generated specifications
that are syntactically accepted by the ACSL/Frama-C front end;
\item \textbf{Validity rate (Val.):} the percentage of generated
specifications that are syntactically correct and whose generated clauses are
accepted by Frama-C/WP under the verifier configuration in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
For benchmarks that contain pre-existing assertions or verification targets,
those targets are excluded from Val.; Val. measures whether the generated
specification itself can be verified. For example, generated loop invariants are
valid only if they satisfy both the \textit{Base} and \textit{Preservation}
conditions;
\item \textbf{Accuracy rate (Acc.):} the percentage of generated specifications
that are syntactically correct, valid, and strong enough to prove the benchmark
verification goal. For pre/postconditions, this means that they imply the given
target assertions (e.g., a functional property); for loop invariants, it further
requires the \textit{Termination} condition.
\end{itemize}
These metrics are assessed under Pass@k: a benchmark is counted as successful if
any of up to $k$ complete generation attempts satisfies the reported metric.
Each attempt is a complete pipeline run and may include syntax repair,
verification, the internal refinement loop, and pruning; refinement iterations
are not counted as additional Pass@k attempts. The Pass@1 cost measurements for
the goal-independent study are reported separately in Section~\ref{subsec:rq7-cost}.











Table~\ref{tab:performance_rates_model} presents a comprehensive overview of our experimental results.

The observed gaps among different evaluation metrics reveal important characteristics of both benchmark difficulty and model capability. As benchmark complexity increases, the discrepancies between Syn., Val., and Acc. become more pronounced. A similar trend is observed across models: weaker models tend to exhibit larger gaps between these metrics. The starkest instance is GPT-3.5-turbo on \textbf{list-S}, where 70.83\% syntactic correctness collapses to 20.83\% validity and only 8.33\% accuracy at Pass@1 --- the model emits well-formed linked-list annotations that the verifier rejects --- whereas Claude~3.7~Sonnet on the same benchmark reaches 95.83\%/83.33\%/75.00\%. Notably, Val. does not trivially coincide with Syn. in our results, indicating that the generated specifications are not simplistic invariants (e.g., \texttt{true}) or trivial properties that programs would satisfy by default. Instead, our approach aims to capture non-trivial functional relations beyond syntax-valid or vacuous annotations, which naturally induces a meaningful distinction between syntactic correctness and semantic validity.

Importantly, our method demonstrates consistent effectiveness across different models and reduces the performance gap between stronger and weaker ones in several benchmark families. For example, on benchmarks such as \textbf{SV-COMP}, \textbf{NLA}, and \textbf{numer-S}, GPT-4o and Claude achieve identical performance at higher Pass@k levels (Pass@3 and Pass@5), suggesting that symbolic guidance can improve the usefulness of comparatively weaker models. Furthermore, the method exhibits rapid convergence under Pass@k evaluation. Performance improvements from Pass@3 to Pass@5 are marginal across several benchmarks, including \textbf{NLA}, \textbf{numer-S}, and \textbf{pIp}.

The results also indicate comparatively lower accuracy on the \textbf{OOPSLA}, \textbf{SV-COMP}, and \textbf{Frama-C} benchmarks. For \textbf{OOPSLA} and \textbf{SV-COMP}, this is primarily due to the presence of nested and multiple loops, where symbolic execution often fails to derive sufficiently strong preconditions. In addition, the absence of explicit loop conditions in \textbf{OOPSLA} further complicates invariant inference. In the case of \textbf{Frama-C}, the difficulty mainly stems from complex loop structures involving array manipulations, external function calls, and recursive behaviors, which remain challenging for symbolic execution. Despite these obstacles, our method still achieves notable performance on these challenging benchmarks. Moreover, the improvements from Pass@1 to Pass@5 show that additional attempts can help recover from difficult generation cases under the evaluated budget.

\begin{finding}
\textbf{Finding (RQ2).} Syntactic correctness is generally high, but the gap between validity and accuracy widens with benchmark complexity and narrows with model capability; \toolname\ narrows model gaps on several benchmark families and reaches most Pass@$k$ gains by Pass@3, with most residual accuracy loss on nested/multi-loop and memory-heavy families.
\end{finding}
