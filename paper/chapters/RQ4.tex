Against numerical-invariant tools, \toolname\ is on par with CLAUSE2INV on
SyGuS, exceeds LIPuS but trails CLAUSE2INV by 6.6\,pp on NLA, and reaches
87\% on nested/multiple-loop OOPSLA. These tools specialize in numerical
loops; \specgen\ is excluded from this C/ACSL table because it targets
Java/JML.

For the C/ACSL LLM baselines, the matched-model comparisons show where the
gains arise. On GPT-4o, \toolname\ reaches 99.2\% on SyGuS versus 86.5\% for
\autospec\ and 79.9\% for ACInv; on OOPSLA it reaches 76.1\%, matching ACInv
and exceeding \autospec's 71.7\%. SV-COMP remains difficult: \toolname\ and
ACInv both reach 76.2\% on GPT-4o. \toolname\ generally gains more from
stronger models than \autospec, whose results vary little or decline on
several families.

The largest differences occur on programs with pointers and data structures.
On pIp, \toolname\ reaches 88.0\% with Claude 3.7 Sonnet versus \autospec's
4.0\%; the latter often emits invalid ACSL, while symbolic-state-derived
skeletons constrain both syntax and memory relations. On linked-list list-S,
\toolname\ reaches 95.8\% with Claude, whereas \autospec\ remains at 0\%.
Inductive-predicate support also helps express properties beyond the finite
symbolic-memory model in the Frama-C and list-S cases.

\begin{finding}
\textbf{Finding (RQ4).} \toolname\ matches tools specialized for numerical invariants and clearly outperforms LLM-based generators (\autospec, ACInv), with the largest margins on the pointer-, struct-, and linked-list families that prior LLM tools handle poorly; its accuracy also scales with model capability where \autospec's plateaus.
\end{finding}
