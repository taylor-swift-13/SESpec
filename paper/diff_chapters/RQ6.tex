\subsection{RQ6: How strong are \toolname's specifications versus the baselines?}
\label{subsec:rq6-strength}

\rev{Validity certifies that a specification is discharged by its verifier, but it does not rank how informative it is. We first compare accepted input domains (preconditions), guarantees on return (postconditions), and predicates at corresponding loop points (loop invariants) independently. More importantly, we then compare the joint contract formed by pre- and postconditions, using the standard refinement order: one contract is stronger if its accepted input domain is no smaller and its guarantees on return are no weaker over the common input domain, with at least one strict advantage.}

\smallskip
\noindent\rev{\textbf{LLM judge and expert review.}}
\rev{Separate judge calls compare preconditions by accepted-domain inclusion, postconditions by bidirectional implication over their common input domain, and invariants at corresponding loop points. Fig.~\ref{fig:fc_vs_judge_strength} also combines the first two dimensions into a contract comparison. Equality requires equality in both the pre-/post-conditions. Missing clauses mean \texttt{true}; unestablished implications count as Incomp. Expert review corrected 32 verdicts. \appref{app:strength} reports these corrections, prompts, reviewer details, and an A/B-order audit.}

\rev{Fig.~\ref{fig:fc_vs_judge_strength} shows stronger \toolname\ postconditions and loop invariants. Whereas \autospec\ accumulates sampled, verifier-accepted clauses, including redundant or tautological ones, \toolname\ supplies local symbolic states to guide path-sensitive and inductive predicates.}

\rev{\autospec\ accepts broader input domains more often (290 versus 96 pairs), while \toolname\ has stronger postconditions (342 versus 18) and loop invariants (120 versus 52) more often. In the combined precondition--postcondition contract comparison, \toolname\ leads in 172 pairs versus 64 for \autospec\ after expert review; WP finds 168 versus 58.}

\yfp{Precondition permissiveness measures input-domain inclusion, not runtime safety. Omitting necessary pointer-validity, bounds, or arithmetic constraints can admit inputs that trigger runtime errors. \toolname\ makes these requirements explicit through symbolic execution, so its more restrictive preconditions can serve a safety purpose.}

\rev{Against \specgen\ (Java/JML), \toolname\ leads on loop invariants (183 versus 9), while \specgen\ leads on postconditions (244 versus 100) and combined contracts (186 versus 92). The swapped-order contract counts are tied (129 versus 129); \appref{app:strength} gives the full cross-language comparison.}

\smallskip
\noindent\rev{\textbf{Frama-C/WP cross-check.}}
\rev{For the 698 pairs in which both \toolname\ and \autospec\ produce valid specifications, we use Frama-C/WP to check implications between their preconditions and postconditions and combine the results to compare the joint contracts. The checks use Frama-C 27.1, Alt-Ergo and Z3, with a five-second timeout per proof obligation. \toolname\ has stronger contracts in 168 pairs, \autospec\ in 58, and 183 pairs are equal. The remaining 289 pairs fall into Other, including conflicting precondition--postcondition directions or unproved implications.}

\rev{WP assigns a direction only when it proves every corresponding
obligation. An unproved implication enters Incomp whether it is false or true
but beyond the prover; unsupported harnesses and unresolved loop
correspondence also enter Incomp. Timeouts and automation limits therefore
leave many invariants unordered. The judge followed by expert review can
inspect semantic relations in these unresolved pairs, while WP provides an
independent formal check. Fig.~\ref{fig:fc_vs_judge_strength} confirms the
precondition and postcondition trends, but WP orders fewer invariants.}
